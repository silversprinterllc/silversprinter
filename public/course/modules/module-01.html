The Three-Layer Market Framework

Learning Objective Score any STR market across all three layers — demand, supply, and regulatory risk — and know before looking at a single property whether this market is worth your capital.

In Module 00, you mapped the Hub-and-Spoke model and built your Business Identity Statement. You identified your property type, your target guest, and your eight demand channels. Now the question is: does the market you're evaluating actually support that model?

Most STR operators pick a property before they pick a market — they fall in love with a cabin, a lakehouse, or a beach cottage and then look for data to justify the decision they've already made emotionally. The result is properties that underperform projections in soft demand periods, get squeezed when supply increases, or lose their permits when the regulatory environment tightens.

Market selection comes first. Property comes second. The market determines what a good property can earn. A mediocre property in a strong market consistently outperforms a great property in a weak one.

📊 Reading Your Score

85–100: Strong market. Proceed to property analysis. The market supports your Hub-and-Spoke model on all three layers.

65–84: Viable market with identifiable weaknesses. Know which layer scored low and what that means for your operating strategy before you proceed. A Layer 2 weakness means you need to out-compete on operations. A Layer 1 weakness means you need off-season revenue strategies from launch.

Below 65: Proceed only if you have a specific, documented edge that compensates for the weakness — a unique property, a locked-in permit in a capped market, a demand-driver insight the data doesn't yet capture. Do not proceed on hope.

Layer 3 score below 10: Stop. A regulatory environment that can revoke your permit erases all Layer 1 and Layer 2 upside. Markets with moratoriums, hard enforcement, or active restriction proposals are markets where you are operating on borrowed time.

How to Pull Layer 1 & 2 Data — AirDNA

AirDNA (airdna.co) is the primary source for Layer 1 and Layer 2 data. This is one of the specific contexts where subscribing for a single month — running your full market analysis — and then canceling makes sense. Do not pay for it monthly as an ongoing tool until you're at the Tier 3 growth stage described in Module 02.

Market Overview. In AirDNA, search your target city, zip code, or neighborhood. The Market Overview gives you the market-wide occupancy rate, ADR, RevPAR, and active listing count for the past 12 months. This is your Layer 2 supply/demand baseline. Download the chart to CSV so you have the data in a spreadsheet, not just a screenshot that disappears when your subscription lapses.

Filter to your comp set. In Rentalizer or the Market Explorer, filter by: property type (house, condo, cabin), bedroom count matching the property you're evaluating, and neighborhood if the market is large enough to have meaningful sub-areas. The filtered view gives you the realistic comp set for your specific property — not the market average, which blends studios and 8-bedroom estates.

Pull the seasonality curve. AirDNA's occupancy chart by month shows you the demand pattern across 12 months. Screenshot and note: when does occupancy peak, when does it trough, and how deep is the trough? This curve is the foundation of your revenue projection in Lesson 3. A trough below 40% for two or more consecutive months requires a specific plan — not a hope that your listing will perform differently from the market baseline.

Check top performer data. AirDNA shows individual listing performance for top earners in the market. Filter to the top 10–20% of listings in your comp set and note: what ADR are they achieving? What occupancy are they maintaining? What is their average star rating? These are the legitimate ceiling benchmarks for your own projections — not aspirational, but evidence-based.

Note the YoY supply trend. In the Market Overview, look at active listing count for the past 24 months. Calculate the annual growth rate: (current listings − listings 12 months ago) ÷ listings 12 months ago = YoY growth %. A market growing faster than 10% year-over-year is being flooded. Score it accordingly in Layer 2.

AirDNA does not tell you anything about the regulatory environment. This layer is manual research and it cannot be skipped. The steps are in Lesson 4 of this module. For scoring purposes: do this before you score Layer 3, not after.

Instructor Case Study

Finger Lakes, New York — Three-Layer Score

Layer 1 — Demand: Multiple complementary demand drivers — Seneca Lake wine trails (dozens of wineries), Watkins Glen State Park (gorge hiking), Watkins Glen International race track (events that drive weekend spikes), and direct proximity to both the Finger Lakes Trail system and regional food/restaurant scene. Seasonal profile is 8–9 strong months (spring through fall), with winter occupancy compressed but not dead — a shoulder season that well-positioned properties can monetize with "cozy lake cabin" positioning. Drive market from multiple major metros: New York City (4.5 hours), Philadelphia (4 hours), Pittsburgh (3.5 hours), Toronto (3 hours). Full 15 points on demand drivers, 10 on seasonality, 10 on drive-market. Layer 1: 35/40.

Layer 2 — Supply: A fragmented comp set — many owner-operated properties with inconsistent presentation, uneven guest communication, and below-average photography. This created an immediate competitive opportunity: operational excellence and professional hospitality in a market where the standard was low. Supply growth has been moderate, not explosive — existing STR operators in the area have not faced the saturation dynamics of Smoky Mountains or coastal Florida markets. Layer 2: scored well at evaluation time.

Layer 3 — Regulatory: New York has county-level STR regulation, not statewide. Schuyler County's regulatory environment at time of investment was stable with no active restriction proposals. The key due diligence: check the specific town or village within the county — some Finger Lakes municipalities have passed their own STR rules that differ from the county framework. Always research at the municipal level, not just the county level.

What this revealed about the Hub-and-Spoke fit: The wine trail connection created a ready-made Local Guide spoke. The gorge hiking drove a specific guest profile (active couples and small groups, not large family groups) that informed the property staging and bedroom configuration choice. Market selection shaped every subsequent decision.

Property Analysis — The STR-Specific Checklist

Learning Objective Evaluate any property against the specific criteria that drive STR performance — criteria a residential buyer, a realtor, or a bank appraisal will not assess for you.

A property that is excellent by residential standards can be mediocre as an STR. A property that seems odd or unfinished by residential standards can be exceptional as an STR. The difference comes down to criteria that are invisible to a market-rate appraisal.

The STR-specific checklist below evaluates six categories. For each property you evaluate, score every item. A property that fails more than two categories in the first three sections requires a documented reason to proceed — not momentum, a specific compensating factor.

Category 1 — Location Within the Market

Not all addresses in a market perform equally. A property 20 minutes from the lake in a "lake house" market earns materially less than one with water access or water views. Within a given market, location within the market is the highest-leverage factor in the property's ceiling.

Location FactorHigh-ValueWatch For
Proximity to primary demand driver Walking distance or direct water/trail/slope access. The guest's primary reason for the trip starts at the property, not from the parking lot. More than 20 minutes from the primary driver. "5 minutes from the lake" is true — for a car. For guests who came for the lake, it means a daily logistics hurdle.
Street access and parking Off-street parking for the number of guests the property sleeps. In a 6-guest property, guests may arrive in 2–3 cars — all need to park. Street-only parking in an area with overnight parking restrictions. Verify this before purchase — a parking complaint from a neighbor is a noise complaint with wheels.
Walkability to amenities Restaurants, shops, or activity rental within walking distance. Adds guest convenience and reduces the "we can't do anything without driving" friction in reviews. Complete car-dependence in markets where guests expect to be able to walk to dinner after checking in.
Neighbor proximity Reasonable distance from neighbors — enough that a quiet gathering doesn't become a shared wall problem. Rural or semi-rural positioning creates natural buffer. Zero-lot-line urban property or condo where sound travels through walls and floors. Noise complaints in dense buildings are common and very difficult to control. Verify HOA rules before proceeding.

Category 2 — Layout and Configuration

Guest capacity is the primary revenue lever in STR. A property that sleeps 8 at an ADR of $300 earns more than a property that sleeps 4 at $250 — even when the per-guest cost is similar. But layout configuration matters as much as raw bedroom count.

Configuration FactorHigh-ValueWatch For
Bedroom-to-bathroom ratio 1 full bathroom per 2 bedrooms is the minimum. Properties with 1:1 ratios command premium ADRs and generate fewer negative review mentions of "everyone fighting for the bathroom." 3+ bedrooms sharing a single bathroom. At full occupancy, this becomes the friction point guests mention in reviews. Hard to fix without a renovation budget.
Sleeping capacity vs. listed capacity Real beds (king, queen, twin) in properly sized rooms with closet or storage for a 2–4 night stay. Guests sleep better, review better, and return. Sleeping capacity inflated by pull-out sofas and air mattresses. Guests know. They note it in reviews: "the kids slept on the air mattress" is not the experience that generates 5 stars.
Common area square footage Kitchen, dining, and living area sized to accommodate the maximum guest count comfortably. A 10-guest property needs a table that seats 10 and a living room where all 10 can sit simultaneously. Bedroom count added to hit a higher maximum but common areas not expanded. Guests at capacity feel cramped — and they write about it.
Outdoor space A deck, patio, fire pit, or yard that extends the usable square footage in favorable weather. In STR, outdoor space with a fire pit, hot tub, or water access is often the primary differentiator in a comp set. No outdoor space at all, or outdoor space that is shared with other units/tenants and cannot be marketed as private.

✓ The Hub-and-Spoke Minimum

A property that supports fewer than 5 spokes at launch is not disqualified — but you need to document which spokes are missing and why, and have a plan for how your owned demand strategy compensates. The Hub-and-Spoke model's power comes from having more than one channel generating demand. A property with only Airbnb and VRBO is a property where a platform algorithm change can cost you 30% of your bookings overnight.

Category 4 — Operational Risk

Before you commit to a property, identify the operational risks you'll be managing from day one. These are not reasons not to buy — they are things you need a plan for before the first guest arrives, not after.

  • Who does the cleaning? Is there a reliable STR-experienced cleaner within 20 minutes of the property? A property that is 45 minutes from any available cleaner creates a logistics problem on same-day turnovers. Call local STR operators in the market and ask who they use — this is the fastest research.
  • What is the maintenance situation? Older properties, properties with unique systems (well and septic, wood stove, boat dock), or properties with known deferred maintenance create a higher operational surface area. The per-booking cost of a HVAC emergency, a septic pump-out, or a dock replacement belongs in your expense model — not as a surprise.
  • What is the internet situation? Rural or remote properties often have limited broadband options. STR guests in 2025 expect at minimum 100 Mbps download. Check at the address: use the FCC broadband map at broadbandmap.fcc.gov or call the local ISP for availability before you close. A property with no broadband option above 25 Mbps will generate guest complaints regardless of how beautiful it is.
  • Is there cell coverage at the property? Guests need to reach you. You need to reach guests. In remote markets, confirm that the major carriers have coverage at the property address — not at the nearest town, at the property. A guest who cannot reach you during their stay is a guest who writes a frustrating review about feeling disconnected and unsupported.

Revenue Underwriting — The Numbers Before You Commit

Learning Objective Build a 12-month revenue projection and full operating expense model for any STR property — so you know the deal's actual economics before emotion enters the equation.

Revenue underwriting is the step that most first-time STR operators skip. They look at an AirDNA "estimated annual revenue" number, apply a rough estimate for expenses, and build their investment thesis on a best-case projection without stress-testing it.

This lesson walks you through the math explicitly. Every number in your projection is sourced, not estimated. The 12-month grid below is not a template you fill in with hopes — it is a model that forces you to be specific about how many nights you are projecting, at what rate, in each month of the year.

The Four Numbers That Define STR Performance

📊 The Core Metrics — Memorize These

ADR (Average Daily Rate): Total revenue ÷ total nights booked. The average nightly rate you actually achieved — not what you listed at, what you received. Pull your comp set's ADR from AirDNA's filtered Market Explorer. The top 20% of comps sets your realistic ceiling. The market average sets your baseline.

Occupancy Rate: Nights booked ÷ nights available × 100. Your market's occupancy rate from AirDNA Layer 2 is the baseline. You should project 10–15% above market average in your first year only if you have a specific, documented reason — not because you plan to work hard.

RevPAR (Revenue Per Available Room): ADR × Occupancy Rate. This is the single number that lets you compare two properties with different pricing and occupancy profiles. A property at $200 ADR and 70% occupancy has a RevPAR of $140 — the same as a property at $280 ADR and 50% occupancy. They look different; they earn the same.

Gross Annual Revenue: ADR × Occupied Nights. The total before platform fees, operating expenses, and taxes. This is the top-line number. Everything below it reduces it.

Building the 12-Month Projection

Use the seasonality curve you pulled from AirDNA in Lesson 1. For each month, you need three data points: projected occupancy %, projected ADR, and projected nights booked. Use your comp set's historical data as the baseline. Do not use your own aspirational projections as a starting point — start with what the market's comparable listings actually achieved, then identify whether you have a specific competitive advantage that justifies projecting above that baseline.

⚠ The Three Most Common Projection Errors
  • Projecting top-performer ADR at market-average occupancy. Top ADR and high occupancy are negatively correlated — when you price high, you fill fewer nights. If you use the top-20% comp's ADR, use their occupancy rate too, not the market average. They are a package.
  • Ignoring the off-season trough entirely. Projecting only peak and shoulder months because the off-season numbers are "too depressing" produces a projection that is 30–40% above actual annual revenue. A deal that works at peak does not guarantee a deal that works across the full year.
  • Forgetting the new-listing discount period. Airbnb's algorithm prioritizes new listings — briefly. To accelerate reviews in your first 60 days, you should price 10–15% below market to generate volume. This suppresses your actual ADR below projection for the first 2 months. Build it into the model, not into a footnote.

The Deal Math

📊 From Gross Revenue to Net Operating Income

Gross Annual Revenue (from your 12-month projection)
minus Platform Fees
= Net Revenue

Net Revenue
minus All Operating Expenses (cleaning, supplies, utilities, insurance, maintenance reserve, tech, permit, taxes)
= Net Operating Income (NOI)

NOI
minus Debt Service (mortgage principal + interest)
= Cash Flow Before Income Tax

For acquisition underwriting: NOI ÷ Purchase Price = Cap Rate. In STR, a cap rate of 8–12% is a functioning investment. Below 6%, you need appreciation expectations to justify the deal. Above 12% in a stable market, double-check the projection — a number that looks too good usually is.

Setting Your Minimum Revenue Threshold

Before you look at the deal emotionally, define your minimum revenue threshold — the annual gross revenue below which you do not proceed regardless of how much you love the property. The formula:

Minimum Revenue = Total Annual Operating Expenses + Debt Service + (Target Annual Cash Flow)

Write this number down before you look at projections. Once you have an emotional investment in a property, your brain will find a way to make the numbers work. Setting the threshold before the analysis insulates the decision from post-hoc rationalization.

Regulatory Due Diligence and the Acquisition Decision

Learning Objective Complete your regulatory research, understand the four permit risk types, and document your final acquisition decision with every variable accounted for.

Regulatory due diligence is where many STR investments quietly fail before they begin — not because the operator skipped it entirely, but because they did surface-level research and got a false sense of security. "I checked and there's no ban" is not due diligence. "I spoke to the municipal clerk, pulled the current active permit count, verified the zoning allows STR, confirmed the HOA has no restrictions, and read the last 12 months of council meeting minutes" is due diligence.

This lesson gives you the specific research steps. None of them are optional if you are committing capital to this market.

The Four Regulatory Risk Types

Permit Cap / Hard Limit
Medium Risk

A finite number of permits issued per municipality. If you can get one now, operating inside a cap is actually protective — new supply is limited by design. The risk: if your permit is revoked, re-entry may require a waitlist. Verify the current cap, current active permits, and the waitlist depth before assuming you can simply reapply if something goes wrong.

Moratorium / Freeze
High Risk

No new permits until further notice. Typically enacted after a community concerns period and before new legislation passes. If the property you're evaluating is in a moratorium area, it is not investable for new STR operators. Check if there are any "grandfathered" operating properties and whether that status transfers with sale — in most jurisdictions it does not.

Owner-Occupancy Requirement
High Risk

Some municipalities require the STR operator to be the primary resident — you can rent the home while you travel but not as an investment property. If you are purchasing an investment STR in a market with an owner-occupancy rule, this is a disqualifying condition. Verify specifically, not generally.

HOA Restriction
High Risk

HOA covenants, conditions, and restrictions (CC&Rs) can prohibit short-term rentals entirely — regardless of whether the municipality permits them. HOA rules are private contracts and they operate independently of municipal permit systems. Pull the CC&Rs and HOA meeting minutes before making an offer on any property in an HOA community.

The Regulatory Research Process — Step by Step

Identify the governing authority. In the U.S., STR regulation is typically handled at the city, town, or county level — sometimes by overlapping jurisdictions. For a rural lakehouse, you may need to check: the state (if NY or CA with statewide frameworks), the county, and the town or village. All three may have different rules. Start at the lowest municipal level and work up.

Search the municipality's website for "short-term rental" or "vacation rental." Most municipalities that regulate STR now have a dedicated page. Read the current ordinance — not a summary, the actual document. Look for: permit requirements, cap or no cap, owner-occupancy requirement, renewal frequency, grounds for revocation, and any inspection requirements. If the website is not clear, call the municipal clerk's office. This is a public record inquiry — they are required to answer.

Verify current permit availability. Ask the municipal clerk: How many STR permits are currently active? Is there a cap? Is there a waitlist? How long does it take to issue a permit after application? Some municipalities process in 2 weeks; others have a 6-month review process. If you are buying a property that is already operating as an STR, confirm the permit is current and transferable — not all are. Some permits are tied to the individual operator, not the property address.

Read the last 12 months of city/town council meeting minutes. Most municipalities post meeting minutes publicly. Search for "short-term rental" or "vacation rental" in those minutes. Look for: any agenda items proposing new restrictions, complaints from neighbors cited in public comment, enforcement actions discussed, or new studies commissioned. A market where STR is appearing on the council agenda with negative framing is a market where the regulatory environment is trending restrictive — regardless of what the current ordinance says.

Pull the zoning designation for the specific address. An STR permit may be available in the municipality but your specific property may be in a zoning district where it is not allowed. Commercial-adjacent or industrial-adjacent zones sometimes have different rules. Search the municipal GIS map or parcel viewer for the specific address and confirm the zoning classification explicitly permits short-term rentals.

Pull the HOA CC&Rs if applicable. If the property is in any type of planned development, community association, or condo association: request the full CC&Rs from the seller's agent or directly from the HOA management company. Search the document for "short-term rental," "vacation rental," "transient occupancy," and "minimum lease term." A "minimum 30-day lease term" clause in the CC&Rs prohibits Airbnb and VRBO-type rentals. This language is increasingly common and was often added without existing homeowners noticing it during an ordinance update vote.

Search for any open enforcement actions or complaints at the address. If you are buying a property that is already operating as an STR, check whether there are any open noise complaints, code violations, or pending enforcement actions attached to the address. Most municipalities maintain this as a public record through their code enforcement or building department. A property with an open complaint may have conditions attached to its permit renewal.

⚠ The New York State STR Landscape — Finger Lakes Specific

New York State passed legislation in 2023 that authorizes (but does not require) municipalities to regulate STR. This means regulation varies significantly town-by-town within the same county. Within the Finger Lakes region, some towns have passed their own STR ordinances while neighboring towns have none. Before purchasing any property in the Finger Lakes, you must research at the town or village level — not at the county level. Schuyler County allows its constituent towns to set their own rules, and they differ. The town of Hector (on Seneca Lake's east side), the Town of Reading (west side), and the Town of Dix (Watkins Glen area) each have different ordinance histories. Research the specific parcel's governing municipality.

Direct Booking Machine

How Property Selection Affects Your Direct Booking Ceiling

The property you acquire determines not just its OTA performance — it determines the ceiling on your direct booking channel. A property with a distinctive identity (a memorable name, a recognizable setting, a clear guest profile) builds word-of-mouth and repeat bookings faster than a generic listing. Guests who remember a property come back. Guests who remember a property tell their friends.

  • Repeat-visitor potential — properties near annual events, recurring seasonal draws, or with a strong emotional identity have naturally higher past-guest return rates. This is your direct booking machine's primary feed: guests who want to come back.
  • Guest profile alignment — a property that attracts a consistent guest type (families, couples, extended-stay professionals) builds a targetable past-guest list faster than one with a scattered demographic. Consistency of guest type makes your Kit email campaigns dramatically more effective.
  • Direct booking economics at underwriting — your underwriting projections should include a Year 3 scenario where 20–30% of bookings are direct, eliminating the 15.5% Airbnb host fee on those nights. That fee elimination should appear as revenue in your long-term IRR calculation.

The acquisition decision you're making now is also a decision about the quality of the direct booking asset you'll own in three years. Choose accordingly.
