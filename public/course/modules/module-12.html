Lesson 1

The Brand Partnership Landscape for STR

Lesson ObjectiveUnderstand why brands want placement in STR properties, identify the five partner categories that work at every property size, and determine whether your property has reached the threshold where this channel is worth activating.

The most common mistake at this stage is using energy from Property 1's success to acquire Property 2 before Property 1 is fully optimized. A property at 60% of its revenue potential with a second property on the way is two properties at 60%. A property at 90% of its revenue potential with a second property in acquisition is one property funding the second.

A $90,000/year property at 60% efficiency is earning $54,000. That's a detail for Module 13. This module is about a revenue layer that runs alongside your existing operations — brand partnerships that turn the hospitality you're already providing into a paid placement channel.

A $90,000/year property at 60% efficiency is earning $54,000. At 90% efficiency, it earns $81,000. That $27,000 gap — achievable with systems already built in this course — is roughly the annual carry cost on a $400,000 second property. Closing the gap on Property 1 often creates the cash flow that makes Property 2 possible without additional out-of-pocket capital.

Why Brands Want STR Placement — The Media Platform Argument

Your average STR property hosts 160–230 nights per year. Each stay involves 2–3 guests who are, by definition, a self-selected demographic: people who travel, who value hospitality-grade experiences, who make deliberate spending decisions. That is a valuable audience. The question brand partnership is asking is: can a brand pay to be associated with the experience you're already delivering?

Hotels have been selling this for decades — branded bath products, co-branded amenity kits, sponsored concierge recommendations. STR hosts can access the same logic at the individual property level. The difference is not scale — it's specificity. A regional wine brand would rather be in 8 Finger Lakes vacation rentals than in a generic hotel chain. Your guests are their target customer, staying in their market, self-selecting the experience.

The Five Brand Categories for STR Partnerships

Not every brand is a fit. The ones that work share a common trait: their product improves the guest experience, so the placement doesn't feel like advertising. It feels like thoughtful hosting.

The Readiness Threshold — When to Activate This Channel

📊 When Brand Partnerships Make Sense

A brand won't send product to a property with no social presence and 40 total nights booked. The minimum signal brands care about:

  • Annual stays: 80+ bookings/year (roughly 120–160 occupied nights). Below this, your reach is too limited for product brands to care.
  • Social presence: An active property account with 300+ followers and consistent posting. Brands will check. A dead account reads as a dead channel.
  • A creator stay on record: Even one piece of quality creator content signals that your property is worth associating with. Brands vet partners the same way you vet creators.
  • Guidebook in place: Brands that want guidebook placement need to see the guidebook exists and is actively delivered to guests.

If you haven't reached this threshold yet: run Modules 07–11 first. The brand partnership channel is the most valuable when you have assets to offer — reach, content, and a system for delivering value.

Lesson 2

Identify & Pitch Sponsors

Lesson ObjectiveBuild a qualified sponsor prospect list, develop your property media kit, and write a pitch email that leads with value — not a feature request. Close your first product-placement deal.

Building Your Prospect List — Local First, Regional Second

Start with brands whose products you already use at your property. The easiest pitch is authentic: "I use your wine for our welcome basket and guests consistently ask about it. I'd love to formalize that relationship." That's not a cold pitch — it's a referral conversation.

  • Walk your welcome sequence: What's in your welcome basket, your guidebook, your bathroom amenity basket? Every product there is a potential partner conversation.
  • Walk your guidebook recommendations: Every local experience, restaurant, or activity you're already recommending is a potential commission partner — they're just not paying you yet.
  • Search "best [product category] in [your market]": Regional winners in wine, spirits, coffee, outdoor gear, and personal care typically have small marketing budgets and huge interest in authentic placement among their target demographics.
  • Look at what other STR hosts in your market feature: Check the guidebooks and welcome kits visible in Instagram posts tagged at nearby properties. Popular brands in your market have already decided they want STR presence.

Qualifying a Prospect — Three Criteria Before You Pitch

Criterion 1: Product-guest fit — Would a guest who stays at your property actually want or use this product? If your guests are outdoor adventurers and you're pitching a luxury perfume brand, the fit isn't there. The pitch only works when the product would feel like thoughtful hosting, not advertising.

Criterion 2: Brand has marketing intent — Does the brand have an Instagram account they post to, a website with a "contact us" or "partnerships" email, any evidence they work with other hospitality or lifestyle accounts? If there's no marketing infrastructure, you're pitching someone who doesn't have a framework to say yes.

Criterion 3: Geographic alignment — Local and regional brands want to reach people in their market. A regional winery wants their bottle in the hands of every couple who visits wine country — that's you. A national brand wants lifestyle placement in aspirational properties — that's also you, at a different scale of relationship.

Your Property Media Kit — What to Include

A media kit for a sponsor is not the same as a creator media kit. Sponsors care about different metrics than creators. Build a one-page document (PDF) with:

  • Property overview: 3–4 sentences. What it is, where it is, what makes it distinctive. No adjectives — facts and differentiators only.
  • Guest profile: Average household income bracket (if known from Airbnb Insights), average travel party size, primary origin markets (states driving your bookings). These are the demographics a brand's marketing team will evaluate.
  • Annual reach numbers: Total occupied nights, estimated unique guests, social media follower count, creator content impressions generated in the past 12 months. Present this as a media buy, not a hospitality relationship.
  • Placement opportunities: Be specific. "Welcome basket placement (seen by every guest on arrival)," "Guidebook feature (read by every guest on arrival and referenced throughout the stay)," "Social mention in post-stay content featuring your product," "Creator content rights (product photographed during influencer stays)."
  • What you're asking for: Be explicit about whether you want product only, product + fee, or an experience/commission arrangement. Ambiguity here loses deals.

✓ Warm Outreach via Creator Network

If a creator who stayed at your property has a relationship with a brand you want to pitch, ask for an introduction. A message that opens with "We hosted [creator] here last spring and they suggested we reach out" is warm outreach, not cold. The creator's existing relationship becomes your credibility. This is one of the compounding benefits of the creator network you built in Module 10 — it doesn't just produce content, it produces relationships.

⚠ When They Say No

Most first pitches get ignored, not refused. If you get a genuine no, ask one question before disengaging: "Is there a better time of year, or a different arrangement that would work for you?" Many brand marketing budgets reset quarterly or annually. A "no in Q3" is often a "yes in Q1" when budget is fresh. Keep a simple tracker: brand name, date pitched, outcome, and follow-up date. One follow-up six months later converts more deals than any first pitch.

Structuring Deals & Delivering Value

Lesson ObjectiveUnderstand the four deal structures available to STR hosts, build the tracking system that proves value to partners, and run the annual renewal conversation that builds a multi-brand sponsor portfolio.

The Four Deal Structures — What Works at Each Stage

Structure 1
Product Only

Brand sends product (quarterly or per-stay). You feature it in the welcome experience, guidebook, and social. No cash changes hands. Most accessible starting point — easy for brands to approve without a budget discussion.

Your cost$0
Your gainProduct + content asset
Brand's approval speedFast
Structure 2
Product + Monthly Fee

Brand sends product AND pays a monthly fee ($50–500/mo depending on your reach and exclusivity). Appropriate for properties with 500+ social followers and consistent creator content. Treat the fee as a marketing line item in the brand's budget — that's how their team will approve it.

Monthly fee range$50–$500
Annual value at $200/mo$2,400
Required social reach500+ followers
Structure 3
Commission / Affiliate

Local experience operators (wineries, boat tours, spas) provide a referral code you share in your guidebook. You earn a commission on every booking your code generates — typically $10–50 per booking. No upfront cost, infinite scalability, naturally incentivized to promote well.

Commission per booking$10–50
Conversion rate (guidebook)10–20%
At 5 partners, 150 guests/yr$750–$3,750/yr

Exclusivity — When It Helps and When It Doesn't

An exclusivity clause makes your partnership more valuable to the brand — and more valuable to you, because you can charge more for it. "Official wine of [Property Name]" or "exclusively featuring [Brand] in-stay" is a premium placement that a brand might pay 2–3× more to secure.

  • When to offer exclusivity: When you have one clearly dominant brand in a category and you want to deepen the relationship. One wine brand, one coffee brand, one outdoor gear partner — exclusive per category, not across all categories.
  • When to avoid exclusivity: When you're not sure a brand will perform. Start non-exclusive, give them one quarter, then upgrade to exclusive if they prove value by sending product consistently and their product resonates with guests.
  • The exclusive pitch: "Right now the arrangement is non-exclusive. Based on how our guests have responded to your product, I'd like to make you our exclusive [category] partner going forward — in exchange for [X adjustment to product supply / fee]."

Proving Value — The Monthly Partner Report

Most brands receive your product in a welcome basket and never hear from you again. The hosts who build lasting partnerships send a monthly value report. It doesn't need to be elaborate — it needs to be consistent:

Stays and impressions this month: Total occupied nights × average guests = total person-impressions of their product. "Your product was experienced by 47 guests this month across 22 stays."

Content documentation: Any photo or reel featuring their product that was created during the month — by you, by guests, or by creators. Attach or link the content. This is the asset a brand's marketing team will circulate internally.

Referral code performance (if applicable): How many times your guidebook's referral code was used. Most experience partners track this and can give you the number — just ask.

Guest feedback (optional but powerful): A one-sentence note from a guest about their product — pulled from a review, a message, or StayFi survey. "A guest this month wrote: 'the wine on arrival was perfect.' Third-party guest validation lands differently than your own promotion of the product."

The Annual Renewal Conversation

Don't wait for a brand to reach out. Every November (or 11 months after signing), schedule the renewal conversation. Come with data:

  • Total stays in the partnership period — the full annual impression count
  • Content produced — total pieces of content featuring their product
  • Referral code revenue generated (if applicable)
  • What's changing next year — your projected stay volume, any new creator relationships, any platform or audience growth
📊 Portfolio Math — What Five Partners Looks Like at Scale

Once you have the system running, the revenue compounds across multiple partnerships:

  • 2 product-only partners (wine brand + candle brand): $0 cash + ~$1,200/yr in product value received
  • 1 product + fee partner (outdoor gear brand at $150/mo): $1,800/yr cash + ongoing product
  • 2 commission partners (local winery + spa at avg $25/booking, 15% conversion rate, 150 stays/yr): $1,125/yr in referral commissions
  • Total portfolio value: $2,925/yr in cash + $1,200/yr in product + compounding content library

This is not the most significant revenue line in the course. But it is the one that costs the least to maintain once set up and that builds brand equity — making your property more attractive to the next creator, the next guest, and eventually, the next brand at a higher rate.

Multi-Property Systems — One Platform Stack, Multiple Properties

Learning Objective Understand how OwnerRez, Kit, StayFi, and your direct booking site extend to a second property without duplicating infrastructure. Know which architecture decisions you make now that determine how well the system scales later.

The infrastructure you built for Property 1 — OwnerRez, Kit, StayFi, the direct booking site — is not single-property infrastructure. Adding Property 2 means connecting a new spoke to the hub, not building a new hub. The labor cost of Property 2 should be a fraction of Property 1's setup, because the system is already running.

This only holds if the system architecture is intentional. A Kit account that wasn't tagged by property, a direct booking site that was designed for one property only, or an OwnerRez channel manager that wasn't configured to handle multiple properties all create friction when Property 2 arrives. The decisions below cost nothing to make correctly now and are expensive to undo later.

OwnerRez — Multi-Property Architecture

OwnerRez is built for multi-property operators. Adding a second property is a matter of configuring a new property in your existing account — the channel manager connections, the trigger messages, the tax settings, and the rate rules all travel with each property as separate configurations under the same account.

  • Trigger messages (guest communications): In OwnerRez, trigger messages are set up per-property. Your pre-arrival message, check-in instructions, mid-stay check-in, and review request sequence from Property 1 become the template — duplicated for Property 2 with property-specific fields updated. Build Property 1's trigger message sequence as a clean, field-driven template from the start so it copies cleanly.
  • Tax settings: Each property has its own tax configuration, reflecting its own jurisdiction's TOT rate, county rate, and state rate. If Property 2 is in a different municipality, it gets a different tax configuration without affecting Property 1's settings.
  • Rate rules and pricing: OwnerRez rate rules are per-property. PriceLabs or Wheelhouse integrations (dynamic pricing tools) connect at the property level, so Property 2 can be connected to the same dynamic pricing account with its own market data.
  • Extras (upsells): OwnerRez Extras are configurable per-property. The early check-in, late checkout, and add-on packages from Property 1 are the template — replicate and adjust pricing for Property 2's market.

Kit — Tagging for Multi-Property Email Lists

Your Kit account becomes more valuable with each property, because every guest from every property joins the same list. A past guest from Property 1 (a lake house in the Finger Lakes) is a qualified prospect for Property 2 (a cabin in the Catskills). The list is a cross-property asset.

The tagging architecture is what makes this work without creating confusion:

  • Tag by property at the StayFi level: StayFi allows you to create a separate WiFi network configuration per property. Each property's StayFi network passes a property-specific tag to Kit when a guest opts in. This means you can segment your list by which property a guest has stayed at — and send property-specific re-booking offers to the right guests.
  • Tag by stay date: OwnerRez's Kit integration can pass stay date information as tags, allowing you to identify first-time guests vs. returning guests, recent guests vs. guests from 18+ months ago, and guests who stayed in peak season vs. shoulder season.
  • Property-specific sequences vs. shared sequences: Some email sequences are property-specific (the welcome email with property-specific local tips, the pre-arrival email with property-specific check-in instructions). Others are shared across properties (the re-booking offer at Day 21, the annual "we're thinking of you" email in the off-season). Build the shared sequences to be property-agnostic from the start — they scale automatically.

Direct Booking Site — Hub vs. Spoke Architecture

This is the most architecturally significant decision in scaling. You have two paths:

Option A
Multi-Property Hub Site
How it works
  • One domain presents multiple properties (e.g., fingerlakesgetaways.com with Property 1 and Property 2 listed)
  • One OwnerRez booking widget with property selection
  • One GBP, one content hub, one email sender domain
Best when
  • Properties are in the same geographic market or region
  • Your brand is the destination brand, not the individual-property brand
  • You plan to add 3+ properties — a hub site scales better than managing 5 separate domains
Trade-off
  • Each property gets slightly less dedicated SEO focus; the domain authority is shared across the region, not concentrated on a single address
Option B
Separate Domain per Property
How it works
  • Each property has its own domain (e.g., lakehouseonseneca.com and catskillsretreat.com)
  • Separate OwnerRez booking widget per site
  • Separate GBP per property (Google allows one GBP per physical location)
Best when
  • Properties are in different markets with different target audiences
  • Each property has a distinct brand identity (a luxury lake house and a rustic cabin are different products)
  • You have 2–3 properties — manageable at this scale
Trade-off
  • Each domain requires its own content strategy and SEO effort; more domains = more ongoing work
✓ The SpokeBnB Native Architecture

The Hub-and-Spoke model from the beginning of this course maps directly to this decision. The hub is the destination brand — the broader geographic market. Each spoke is a property within that market. A hub site at the destination level (the Finger Lakes, the Smokies, the Outer Banks) with individual property pages is the SpokeBnB architecture applied to your direct booking stack. It scales better than separate domains, concentrates SEO authority, and positions you as a destination operator rather than a single-property host. The Second Property Decision — Same Market or New Market

Learning Objective Make a deliberate, data-driven decision about whether your second property should be in your existing market or a new one — using a structured comparison of the regulatory, operational, and revenue leverage factors on each side.

Most operators pick their second market the same way they picked their first: by feel, by opportunity, or by where they want to vacation. This is how operators end up with two properties in two markets they don't understand deeply, each requiring full build-out of the local knowledge, vendor relationships, and content strategy that already exists for Property 1's market.

The same-market vs. new-market decision deserves the same rigor as the Module 11 market entry checklist. The considerations are different — this is not just a regulatory question but an operational leverage question. The right answer depends on your specific situation, not on a general rule.

The Same-Market Case — Concentrated Leverage

When you add a second property in the same market as Property 1, every asset built for Property 1 becomes more valuable:

  • Regulatory clarity already exists. You know the permit requirements, the tax stack, the HOA landscape for this market. Property 2 in the same market requires a property-level compliance check, not a market-level regulatory build-out from scratch.
  • Your email list already knows this destination. A guest who stayed at your Finger Lakes property and loved it is a warm prospect for your second Finger Lakes property — with a specific offer: "If our lake house was booked when you wanted to visit, we now have a second property available. Same quality, different view." That offer converts well.
  • Your content and GBP already target this market. The hub piece from Module 08 that targets "best lake houses in the Finger Lakes" now promotes two properties with one piece of content. The GBP profile and reviews apply to Properties 1 and 2 under the same destination brand.
  • Your local vendor relationships scale. The same cleaner, the same maintenance vendor, the same local guide partnerships from Module 13 serve both properties. You've already negotiated rates and built trust with these people.
  • The second property gets reviews faster. Your past-guest list is already in the market. A soft launch email to past guests ("We've opened a second property — book direct for our founding guest rate") generates first-week bookings and first reviews without waiting for OTA search traction.

The New-Market Case — Diversification

Adding a second property in a new market is the right call when:

  • Your existing market is saturated or approaching regulatory risk. If the Finger Lakes STR market has tightened significantly, or if your research shows pending restrictions, a second market in a different regulatory environment is genuine diversification.
  • A specific opportunity has appeared that doesn't exist in your current market. A property at a price point, location, or amenity level not available in your market — and you've done the Module 11 research and confirmed it's Green.
  • You're building a portfolio for long-term income diversification rather than maximum short-term leverage. Two properties in different markets means two different guest demographics, two different seasonality patterns, and two different regulatory environments — a genuinely diversified income base.
⚠ The Real Cost of a New Market

A second property in a new market requires the operator to rebuild from zero everything that already exists for Property 1: market regulatory research (Module 11 checklist), a new market's pricing data and seasonality patterns (Module 03), new local vendor relationships, a new content strategy targeting new market keywords (Module 08), a new GBP profile, and potentially a new domain or new property page on the direct booking site. The operational setup cost for Property 2 in a new market is close to the operational setup cost for Property 1. In a same market, it is roughly 30–40% of Property 1's setup cost.

This doesn't mean new markets are wrong. It means the new market decision should be made because the diversification is worth the cost, not because the opportunity looked attractive before the cost was calculated.

The Acquisition Criteria Framework — What Makes a Good Property 2

Regardless of same-market or new-market, a second property evaluated through the SpokeBnB lens looks for:

  • Direct booking potential: Is this a destination-driven property (guests are visiting the area, not just the property) or an amenity-driven property (guests are coming specifically for the hot tub, the lake access, the proximity to a ski resort)? Destination-driven properties build direct booking channels faster because the content and GBP work targets a destination keyword, not just the property.
  • Regulatory Green classification: Run the Module 11 market entry checklist. A Yellow market may still be viable but requires a shorter investment horizon and a stronger emphasis on direct booking infrastructure as a hedge.
  • Upsell and experience partnership opportunity: Does the area have the kind of local operators (guides, chefs, activity providers) who form the Module 10 partnerships? A market with no local experience infrastructure produces lower ancillary revenue.
  • Content and SEO leverage: Does the market have a search-traffic base for STR-related keywords? A market with 100 monthly searches for "vacation rentals in [market]" builds an organic booking channel more slowly than a market with 1,000. AirDNA and SEMrush both provide market-level search volume data.

  • New-market alternative identified if applicable — Module 11 checklist run on the prospective market
  • Acquisition criteria framework applied to any specific property under consideration
  • Decision written down — not "we're thinking about it" but "Property 2 will be in [market] because [specific criteria]"

Building the Team — Moving from Owner-Operator to Operator

Learning Objective Map the complete labor stack of a scaled STR operation, understand the cost and leverage of each hire, and build the SOPs that allow someone else to run your property to your standard without you present.

A single-property STR run by its owner can operate with informal systems — a mental checklist, a text thread with the cleaner, a vendor's cell number saved in your phone. The transition from informal operations to documented systems is not a sign that the business has gotten more complicated. It is the sign that it has become a real business.

The goal of this lesson is not to hire everyone on the labor stack immediately. It is to map the full stack so you know what you're eventually building toward, and to identify the highest-leverage hire for your current stage.

Standard Operating Procedures — What Allows the Team to Run Without You

An SOP is not a bureaucratic document. It is the written version of how you do something when you're doing it well — captured before you hand it off, so the person who receives it can do it the same way you would. Four SOPs make the difference between a scalable operation and one that depends on you being available.

🧹
Cleaning & Turnover SOP
  • Room-by-room cleaning sequence
  • Linen change and staging standard (with photos)
  • Restocking checklist: toiletries, paper goods, coffee, welcome items
  • Damage inspection — what to photograph and report
  • Final walkthrough checklist before marking ready
  • Emergency supply location (extra linens, light bulbs, batteries)
🔍
Pre-Checkout Inspection SOP
  • High-wear item check: HVAC filters, smoke detectors, CO detectors
  • Appliance function test: hot tub temp, fire pit ignition, smart locks
  • Exterior inspection: patio furniture, outdoor items staged correctly
  • Guest supply levels: where below-threshold restocking is needed
  • Damage documentation process: photos, OwnerRez claim notes
🔧
Maintenance & Vendor SOP
  • Vendor list with phone, specialty, and response time expectation
  • Issue classification: what to fix immediately vs. schedule vs. defer
  • Guest-impact issues: who calls the guest if a fix takes longer than expected
  • Utility shutoff locations: water main, electrical panel, gas shutoff
  • Spare key and lock code protocol for vendor access
💬
Guest Communication SOP
  • OwnerRez trigger message sequence — what fires automatically vs. what requires a human response
  • Response time standard: all guest messages within X hours
  • Escalation path: co-host → owner for issues beyond co-host authority
  • Common issue scripts: early check-in requests, lost keys, noise complaints
  • Review request: OwnerRez automated vs. personal follow-up threshold

A traditional OTA-dependent operator adding a second property starts from zero: new listing, no reviews, no ranking, competing against established listings in a new market. The SpokeBnB operator adding Property 2 in the same market starts with an email list of past guests, a domain with existing SEO authority, a GBP with existing reviews, and a Kit automation that can send a direct offer to past guests within 24 hours of Property 2 going live.

Property 2's first booking window looks different for the two operators. The OTA-dependent operator waits weeks for their first review and months for search traction. The SpokeBnB operator emails 150 past guests with a founding-rate offer, gets 10–15 direct bookings in the first two weeks, and Property 2 has its first 10 reviews before it would have even ranked on Airbnb as a new listing.

  • Email list (Module 07) — a soft launch offer to past guests converts at 8–12% (the same re-booking rate as the Day-21 offer). 150 past guests × 10% = 15 advance bookings before the listing goes live.

  • Reviews (Module 09) — the review request sequence from Property 1 is the template for Property 2. Property 2's review velocity starts at the same systematic rate as Property 1's. No learning curve on the process.
