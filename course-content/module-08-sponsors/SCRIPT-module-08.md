### SLIDE 2 — Title: "The Four Types of Partnerships"

**On Screen:**
1. **Free Product** — Brands send product in exchange for placement and exposure
2. **Paid Sponsorship** — Brands pay a monthly/annual fee for premium placement
3. **Affiliate/Commission** — You earn a percentage when guests purchase products
4. **Shoppable Stays** — Guests can buy anything in the property (Minoan model)

**Talking Points:**
There are four distinct ways to structure product placement deals, and they are not mutually exclusive. Most mature STR operators run all four simultaneously.

The first is free product. This is the easiest entry point. A local coffee roaster sends you a case of beans every month. A skincare brand sends their full product line for the bathroom. A cookware company outfits your kitchen. You get premium products at zero cost, which directly reduces your operating expenses and elevates the guest experience. Even if no cash changes hands, free product that replaces stuff you were going to buy anyway is real revenue.

The second is paid sponsorship. This is where a brand pays you a flat monthly or annual fee to feature their products prominently. Think of it like shelf space in a grocery store. Brands pay for prime placement, and your property is the shelf. This works best when you can demonstrate guest volume and demographic data.

Third is affiliate and commission models. You place a product, include a discount code or QR code, and earn a percentage of every sale that traces back to your property. This is performance-based, so brands love it because they only pay when it works.

Fourth is the shoppable stay model, which platforms like Minoan Experience have pioneered. Every item in your property, from the couch to the candles, is available for guests to purchase at a discount. You earn a commission on every sale, and the guest gets to take the vacation home with them. We will do a full walkthrough of Minoan later in this module.

**Talking Points:**
Before we chase national brands and big sponsorship deals, we need to start where the money is easiest: right in your backyard.

Local business partnerships are the fastest path to partnership revenue for three reasons. First, the business owners are accessible. You can walk into a winery or a restaurant and have a conversation with the owner that same day. Try doing that with Casper or Brooklinen.

Third, there is almost no risk for either party. You do not need a contract for a handshake deal where a restaurant gives your guests 10% off and pays you a $50 monthly placement fee. If it does not work, you both walk away.

And here is the strategic reason: every local partnership you close becomes a case study for bigger deals later. When you pitch a national brand in six months, you can say "I currently manage partnerships with 12 local businesses generating $500/month in combined revenue. Here is my guest data." ### SLIDE 3 — Title: "The Partnership Pitch (What to Say)"

**On Screen:**

**The 3-Part Framework:**
1. **Establish credibility** — Who you are, your property stats
2. **State the value** — What you are offering them (qualified customers)
3. **Make the ask** — Simple, low-commitment starting point

**Key Stats to Include:**
- Annual guest-nights
- Average guest demographic
- Your online reach (listing views, social followers, email list)
- Current review score

**Talking Points:**
Your pitch needs to accomplish three things in under two minutes, whether you deliver it in person or via email.

First, establish credibility. You are not some random person asking for free stuff. You are a hospitality business owner with a proven guest pipeline. Lead with your property name, your nightly rate (this signals your guest's income level), your occupancy, and your review score. "I operate Lakeside Landing, a luxury lakefront property on Seneca Lake averaging $1,200 per night with a 4.97 star rating" immediately positions you as a serious business.

Second, state the value clearly. Do not be vague. "I host approximately 150 guest-nights per year, primarily affluent couples and families from New York City and Boston who are actively seeking local dining and experiences." You are telling them exactly who you are sending and how many.

Third, make the ask simple and low-commitment. Never lead with "I want you to pay me." Lead with "I would love to recommend your business to my guests. Can we explore a way to make that mutually beneficial?" The money conversation comes after they say yes to the concept.

### SLIDE 5 — Title: "Deal Structures That Work"

**On Screen:**

| Structure | How It Works | Example | Revenue |
|-----------|-------------|---------|---------|
| **Discount Card** | Guests show card for discount, you track redemptions | "10% off at [Restaurant]" | $0 cash, builds relationship |
| **Commission** | You earn % of every guest purchase | Wine tour books via your link: 15% commission | $20-$50 per booking |
| **Flat Monthly Fee** | Business pays monthly for "recommended" status | Restaurant pays $75/mo for featured placement | $75/month guaranteed |
| **Product Trade** | Free product in exchange for placement | Coffee roaster provides beans, you display their brand | $30-$80/mo in savings |
| **Hybrid** | Combination of above | $50/mo flat + 10% commission on bookings over 5/mo | Varies |

**Talking Points:**
Let me break down the five deal structures I use and when each one makes sense.

Discount cards are your entry point. They cost the partner nothing upfront and give your guests immediate value. "Show this card for 10% off at Stonecat Cafe." No money changes hands between you and the business, but it builds the relationship and demonstrates that you actually send customers. Use this as a foot in the door, then upgrade to a paid structure after you prove value.

Commission deals are my favorite because they are performance-based and scale naturally. If I send a boat charter company five bookings in a month at $300 each, and I earn 15%, that is $225 in one month from one partnership. The business only pays when they get customers, so there is no risk for them.

Flat monthly fees are the most predictable revenue. I have a restaurant that pays $75 per month to be our "Featured Dining Partner" at Lakeside Landing. They get a dedicated card in the welcome binder, a mention in every guest message, and a spot on our website. It is a great deal for them because even one table per month from my referral covers their cost.

Product trade is not cash revenue, but it is real value. My coffee partner sends a case of premium beans every month. That is $40 to $60 in product I no longer purchase. My soap and candle supplier provides bathroom amenities. These savings are functionally identical to revenue.

Hybrid deals combine structures. "$50 per month flat fee plus 10% commission on any bookings above five per month." This gives both sides skin in the game.

## [HOOK — ON CAMERA, 60-90 seconds]

### SLIDE 3 — Title: "Music Licensing: What You Need to Know"

**On Screen:**

**The Legal Landscape:**
- Personal Spotify/Apple Music accounts = licensed for personal use only
- Playing music in a commercial setting (your STR) = technically public performance
- **Low risk reality:** Single-unit STRs playing background music via streaming are extremely low enforcement priority
- **If you want to be fully compliant:**
  - Soundtrack Your Brand (commercial Spotify alternative, ~$35/month)
  - Rockbot or Cloud Cover Music (commercial background music services)
  - Royalty-free music libraries (Artlist, Epidemic Sound, Musicbed)

**Practical Approach:**
- For 1-3 properties: Spotify with a dedicated property account is standard practice
- For 4+ properties / portfolio: Consider a commercial music license
- For content you create (social media, YouTube): Always use licensed music

**Talking Points:**
I need to address music licensing because I want you to be informed, even though this is a gray area that most STR operators navigate the same way.

Technically, playing music via a personal Spotify subscription in a commercial setting like a short-term rental is a public performance, and Spotify's terms of service license it for personal use only. PROs like ASCAP and BMI could theoretically require a commercial license.

In practice, enforcement against single-unit STR operators playing background music through a consumer streaming account is essentially nonexistent. This is the reality. Every hotel with a Bluetooth speaker in the lobby, every Airbnb with a Sonos system, is in the same boat.

That said, if you want to be fully compliant, there are commercial music platforms designed for exactly this purpose. Soundtrack Your Brand is essentially commercial Spotify. It costs about $35 per month and gives you a licensed, curated music platform designed for businesses. Rockbot and Cloud Cover Music are similar.

My practical recommendation: for one to three properties, use a dedicated Spotify account for your property. It is standard practice. If you scale to four or more properties, consider a commercial license to eliminate any risk.

One important distinction: music licensing for content you create is a completely different issue. If you are making Instagram Reels, TikToks, or YouTube videos featuring your property, you must use licensed music. Use Artlist, Epidemic Sound, or the royalty-free libraries available on each platform. Do not use copyrighted music in your marketing content.

### SLIDE 3 — Title: "ROI Calculation for Partners"

**On Screen:**

**For Local Partners (what you provide them):**
```
Guest Referrals: [# of guests sent]
Estimated Revenue Generated: [referrals x avg spend]
Their Investment: [what they pay you/month]
Their ROI: (Revenue Generated - Investment) / Investment x 100

Example:
- You sent 15 guests to Lake Wine Tours in Q1
- Average booking: $180/person
- Estimated revenue generated: $2,700
- They pay you: $225/quarter (15% commission)
- Their ROI: ($2,700 - $225) / $225 = 1,100% ROI
```

**For Brand Partners (what you provide them):**
```
Brand Impression Days: [guest-nights x avg guests per booking]
Cost per Impression Day: [their investment / impression days]
Content Delivered: [# photos, posts, mentions]
Equivalent Ad Spend: [impressions x CPM benchmark]

Example:
- 150 guest-nights x 2.5 avg guests = 375 impression days
- Brand pays $200/month ($2,400/year)
- Cost per impression day: $6.40
- Plus 12 social media features, 4 newsletter mentions
- Equivalent ad spend value: ~$5,000-$8,000
```

**Talking Points:**
Here is where tracking becomes a superpower. When you can calculate and present ROI data to your partners, you completely change the power dynamic.

For local partners, the ROI calculation is straightforward. You know how many guests you referred because you are tracking QR code scans or discount code redemptions. Multiply by the partner's average transaction value, and you have an estimate of the revenue you generated for them. Subtract what they pay you, and that is their ROI.

In the example on screen, I sent 15 guests to a local wine tour company in one quarter. At $180 per booking, that is $2,700 in revenue I generated for them. They paid me $225 in commissions. Their ROI is over 1,100 percent. When I present that number in a review meeting, guess what happens? They want to increase the partnership, not reduce it.

For brand partners, the calculation uses impression days. If you host 150 guest-nights and the average booking has 2.5 guests, that is 375 impression days. If the brand pays you $2,400 per year, their cost per impression day is $6.40. Compare that to what they pay for digital advertising impressions, which might be $15 to $30 per thousand impressions for seconds of attention. Your impression days are hours of attention for $6.40 each. That is an incredible deal for them, and showing them this math keeps them renewing.

### SLIDE 4 — Title: "Quarterly Review Process"

**On Screen:**

**Quarterly Review Cadence (set calendar reminders):**
- **Week 1 of each quarter:** Update tracker with previous quarter's final numbers
- **Week 2:** Review performance by partnership
- **Week 3:** Partner communication (top performers get thank-you + expansion conversation; underperformers get check-in + optimization discussion)

**Quarterly Review Checklist:**
- [ ] All monthly revenue logged and categorized
- [ ] Dashboard summary updated
- [ ] Top 3 and bottom 3 identified
- [ ] ROI reports generated for each partner
- [ ] Renewal dates and rate review dates checked
- [ ] At least 2 partner conversations scheduled

- [ ] Goals set for next quarter (revenue target, new partnerships, upgrades)

**Talking Points:**
A quarterly review cadence is what separates amateur partnerships from a professional revenue stream. Here is exactly how I run mine.

In the first week of each quarter, I close the books on the previous quarter. I update the tracker with all final numbers, reconcile any outstanding commissions, and log product deliveries with their values.

In week two, I review. I identify the top three and bottom three partnerships by revenue. I look at trends. Is a partnership growing or declining? Has a commission partner sent fewer referrals? Has a flat-fee partner gotten better value than expected? I flag any partnerships that need attention.

In week three, I communicate. My top performers get a personal thank-you message and a conversation about expansion. "You were our top partner last quarter. I sent you $4,000 in business. I'd love to discuss featured placement and an increased commission structure." My underperformers get a check-in. "Our partnership generated lower numbers than expected last quarter. Let's discuss how to optimize."

In week four, I do outreach. Armed with fresh data from my current partnerships, I reach out to new potential partners. My pitch is stronger because I have real numbers and real testimonials.

Set calendar reminders for each of these four weeks. The quarterly review takes maybe three to four hours total, and it is the single highest-ROI activity in your partnership business.
