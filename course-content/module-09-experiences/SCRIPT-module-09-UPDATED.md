# LESSON 9.2: Airbnb Experiences — Optional Channel, Not Engine (10-12 min)

*Reframed: a marketing experiment, not a core revenue stream*

---

## [HOOK — ON CAMERA, 60-90 seconds]

"Airbnb Experiences is a real channel. Some operators make good money on it. But I want to be honest about where it fits in the ecosystem model — it's not the engine. It's a side bet. Your owned /packages and /add-ons are the engine. Airbnb Experiences is an optional marketing funnel that can attract people to your brand, but the revenue it generates is capped by Airbnb's 20% commission and the platform's algorithm risk.

This lesson is shorter than the original version of it. I'll walk you through how it works, what it qualifies, and how to submit one. But I want you to spend the bulk of your energy on the owned packages and add-ons in the next few lessons. Those are yours forever. This one belongs to Airbnb."

---

## [SLIDES — SCREEN RECORD WITH FACE CAM, 6-8 minutes]

### Slide 1: How Airbnb Experiences Work

**On-screen:**
- You design an activity — wine tasting, cooking class, guided kayak
- Set price ($30-200/person), group size (2-10), schedule
- Airbnb takes 20% commission
- Airbnb promotes it in their search + email marketing

**Talking points:**
"Standard mechanics. The 20% commission is meaningful — on an owned /add-ons/chef-dinner you're keeping 100% of your margin. On an Airbnb Experience of the same dinner, you're handing 20% to Airbnb. Trade-off: Airbnb gives you marketing reach you don't have on your own."

---

### Slide 2: When Airbnb Experiences Makes Sense

**On-screen:**

Make sense when:
- You have downtime and enjoy hosting
- You have deep local knowledge and want to test it publicly
- You want a marketing funnel that links back to your property listing
- You can co-host with a local pro (chef, sommelier, guide)

Don't make sense when:
- You already have /packages and /add-ons converting well on your own site
- Your time is better spent creating /local-guide content
- You don't want to deal with Airbnb's review and scheduling system

**Talking points:**
"If you've never hosted experiences, submitting one Airbnb Experience is a worthwhile experiment. Treat it as a funnel — guests who book your experience see your host profile, see your property listing, and may book direct next time. If it generates real revenue, great. If not, you've still pulled eyeballs to your brand.

But don't pour hours into building a full Airbnb Experience operation at the expense of your owned /packages and /add-ons. Those are the durable asset."

---

### Slide 3: The Co-Host Model (Briefly)

**On-screen:**
- You list, a local pro delivers
- 60/40 or 70/30 revenue split (you/them) or flat fee ($100-200/session)
- You handle: listing, pricing, scheduling, marketing
- They handle: the actual experience

**Talking points:**
"The co-host model is the only version of Airbnb Experiences I'd seriously recommend for most STR operators. You're not delivering the experience yourself — a local wine educator or chef does. You're just running the listing. This is minimal time investment for a possible revenue stream."

---

### Slide 4: Cross-Promotion Flywheel (the real reason to do it)

**On-screen:**
- Experience listing → host profile → property listing
- Property listing → /about → direct site → /packages
- Every Airbnb Experience booking is a warm lead for your real business

**Talking points:**
"The revenue from the Experience itself is fine. The marketing effect is the real point. Someone books your wine tasting Experience for $95. They loved it. They click your host profile. They see your property. They bookmark it. Three months later, they book direct on your site — potentially adding a /packages/fall-wine-weekend to that booking.

That's the funnel. Airbnb Experiences is a top-of-funnel discovery tool. Your site is where the real conversion happens."

---

## [WALKTHROUGH — SCREEN RECORDING, 4-5 minutes]

**[SCREEN RECORDING: Airbnb Experience submission — abbreviated]**

"I'm going to move fast through this because you may or may not need it.

Step 1: airbnb.com/host/experiences → Create an Experience
Step 2: Choose 'In-person'
Step 3: Write a specific, evocative title — 'Hidden Gems of the Seneca Lake Wine Trail,' not 'Wine Tour'
Step 4: Step-by-step itinerary written as a story
Step 5: 5+ high-quality photos
Step 6: Price, group size, schedule
Step 7: Submit. 5-14 day review.

Most Experiences get approved on first or second try. The bar is specificity. 'A tour of local wineries' gets rejected. 'A hidden gems Riesling journey through three family-owned vineyards with a picnic overlooking Seneca Lake' gets approved."

---

## [CLOSE — ON CAMERA, 60 seconds]

"Airbnb Experiences is optional. If it sparks joy and you have time, submit one — at minimum it's free marketing. But don't treat it as a core revenue stream. Your core revenue is on your site — packages and add-ons you own outright.

Action: decide if you're running an Airbnb Experience or skipping. Either is fine. If you run one, use the co-host model. If you skip, spend those hours on the owned assets in the next lessons."

---

## [HOMEWORK]

2. If running one: draft the listing, identify a co-host, submit for review

# LESSON 9.7 (NEW): Package and Add-On Design Workshop (14-16 min)

*How to design a package that sells at $2,000+*

---

## [HOOK — ON CAMERA, 60-90 seconds]

"Most STR operators price a package as 'room rate + 10%.' That's a discount stack. That's not a package.

A real package is a curated experience that commands a $2,000+ price because the guest is buying a transformation, not a stack of line items. Lakeside Landing's Fall Wine Weekend is $2,200. Comparable room-only nights would run $1,400-1,600 for the same three days. The package commands a $600-800 premium because of what it includes — and how it's presented.

This lesson is the package design workshop. Pricing psychology, margin targets, the pre-arrival upsell sequence, and how to A/B test package performance. By the end, you'll have a framework to design packages that sell."

---

## [SLIDES — SCREEN RECORD WITH FACE CAM, 8-10 minutes]

### Slide 1: What a Real Package Is

**On-screen:**

Not a package: room rate + 10% discount

A real package:
- Has a name that describes the experience ('Fall Wine Weekend,' not 'Three-Night Stay')
- Has a specific guest persona ('for couples celebrating a milestone')
- Includes 3-6 curated elements (accommodation + 2-5 experiences)
- Has a clear timeline ('Friday afternoon through Monday morning')
- Is priced holistically ($2,200 all-in) not line-itemed
- Solves a real planning problem (the guest doesn't have to research anything)

**Talking points:**
"The difference between a package and a discount is positioning. A discount says 'you get less for cheaper.' A package says 'you get more, curated, effortlessly.' Guests pay more for less friction."

---

### Slide 2: Pricing Psychology of Packages

**On-screen:**

Three pricing principles:

1. **The all-in number beats itemization.** $2,200 for a weekend feels different than '$450/night + $150/person chef + $85/person wine tour + $450 boat.' Same total. Different psychology.

2. **Price above round numbers signals quality.** $2,195 feels cheaper than $2,200. $1,995 outperforms $2,000 on conversion by 15-25% in most tests.

3. **Anchor high, anchor early.** Show the premium package FIRST on /packages hub. Mid-tier package next. Budget option last. Guests anchor to the first number they see.

**Talking points:**
"The all-in number is the most important. Guests hate line items. They want to know 'what does this cost' — one number, one answer. Itemized packages feel like a nickel-and-dime scheme even when the total is identical.

Pricing above a round number is table stakes — every DTC brand does it. Apply it here.

Anchoring high is counterintuitive but proven. When a guest sees /packages/reunion-compound first at $8,500, then /packages/fall-wine-weekend at $2,200, the $2,200 feels like a reasonable choice. Reverse the order and $2,200 feels extravagant."

---

### Slide 3: Margin Targets

**On-screen:**

Target margins on packaged experiences: 40-60%

Example — /packages/fall-wine-weekend at $2,200:
- 3 nights at $450/night = $1,350 base room revenue
- Chef dinner for 2 = $300 (cost $250, margin $50)
- Wine tour for 2 = $250 (cost $220, margin $30)
- Welcome gift basket = $50 (cost $30, margin $20)
- Late checkout = $0 (pure profit)
- Package total: $1,950 direct costs, $2,200 price = $250 package markup
- PLUS $100 commission from chef partner + $25 commission from wine tour
- Total margin on package vs. itemized: $250 + $125 = $375 extra / $2,200 = 17% package premium

**Talking points:**
"Don't aim for 40-60% margin on the PACKAGE — aim for 40-60% margin on the ADD-ON components within the package. The base room rate is whatever your normal rate is. The experience components carry the margin.

The $375 in extra margin per sold package compounds fast. 40 packages a year × $375 = $15,000 in extra revenue, ON TOP of the base room rate."
